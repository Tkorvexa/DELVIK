Cladding & Window Details: 7 Things That Help Prevent Leaks

Most leak problems are not dramatic failures. They often begin at small junctions where water, drainage, flashings, clearances and seal lines have not been treated as one complete system.

By Thiago Cortes, Licensed Building Practitioner · Published 24 September 2026 · 8 minute read

A finished wall can look clean while the details that determine long-term performance remain hidden. Around windows and doors, the cladding, cavity, underlay, flashings and joinery need to work together so water is shed, drained and allowed to dry.

This guide explains the practical checks DELVIK looks for when reviewing cladding and external-joinery junctions. It is general information, not a substitute for project-specific consented drawings, specifications, manufacturer requirements or the applicable New Zealand Building Code compliance pathway.

The detail map: what each part is trying to do

01 · Sill

Goal: give water a clear route outward. Avoid creating a hidden dam with sealant, battens or cladding.

02 · Jambs

Goal: keep the side junction coordinated with the underlay, cavity, joinery and cladding system.

03 · Head

Goal: intercept water above the opening and direct it back outside the cladding system.

04 · Cavity

Goal: preserve drainage and drying. Components should not unintentionally block the cavity path.

1. Keep drainage paths open

A drained cavity only performs as intended when moisture can move downward and exit. Cavity battens, flashings, closers, sealants and cladding cuts should be checked as one sequence rather than as isolated components.

  • Confirm the cavity drainage route remains continuous.
  • Check that closures and trims are installed to the specified detail.
  • Do not bridge or seal over intentional drainage openings.
  • Keep the bottom of the cavity free from construction debris and offcuts.

2. Treat the sill as a drainage point

The bottom of a window opening is where water management becomes visible. The exact detail varies by cladding, joinery and consented design, but the principle stays consistent: water that reaches the junction needs a deliberate route back to the exterior.

“Seal every visible gap” is not a reliable rule. Some gaps exist because drainage and ventilation are part of the designed system.

3. Make the head flashing shed water away

The head flashing above an opening should intercept water and direct it away from the window-to-wall junction. Flashing length, end treatment, projection, cladding clearance and the surrounding cavity detail all need to match the approved construction detail.

4. Do not ignore the jambs

Sills and head flashings get most of the attention, but the side junctions can become concealed water paths when the wall underlay, cavity, air seals and cladding interfaces are not coordinated. Review the whole perimeter of the opening, not only the most visible edges.

5. Maintain the specified clearances

Cladding should not simply be fitted as tightly as possible to adjacent elements. Required clearances can provide drainage, drying, movement or separation from moisture-prone surfaces. The correct dimensions depend on the selected cladding system, joinery, project design and compliance pathway.

Use the consented detail and current manufacturer information rather than relying on a remembered dimension from a different system.

6. Use sealant as part of the system — not as a repair strategy

Sealant is important at many envelope junctions, but it should not be used to compensate for poor geometry, missing laps or blocked drainage. A robust detail normally combines flashings, laps, drainage, clearances and seal lines so one component is not carrying the whole risk.

7. Think of cladding and joinery as one weatherproofing system

The window does not weatherproof the building by itself. Neither does the cladding, building wrap or cavity. The performance comes from layers working together:

Cladding

Sheds most rain before it reaches the secondary layers.

Flashings & junctions

Manage concentrated water at openings, penetrations and transitions.

Cavity

Provides a drainage and drying route where the system uses a drained cavity.

Underlay & air seals

Provide additional moisture and air-control functions within the approved wall system.

Three common mistakes to watch for

  • Blocked drainage: a trim, sealant bead or poorly positioned component closes the intended water path.
  • Tight clearances: cladding is installed too close to joinery, flashings or adjacent surfaces for the specified detail.
  • Poor flashing laps: water can track behind an overlap rather than being directed outward.

Why this matters in Tauranga and Papamoa

Bay of Plenty homes can experience wind-driven rain and coastal exposure. That makes disciplined installation and material selection important, but the correct solution still depends on the actual site, exposure, cladding system, joinery and consented design. A generic social-media detail should never override the project documents.

Before cladding goes on: a five-minute site check

  1. Confirm the latest consented detail and product information are on site.
  2. Check underlay and flexible flashing tape around the opening before it is concealed.
  3. Confirm head, sill and jamb components are installed in the intended sequence.
  4. Check cavity drainage and required clearances before fixing the final boards or panels.
  5. Photograph critical junctions before they become hidden.
